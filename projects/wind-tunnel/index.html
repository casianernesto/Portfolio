Small Scale Wind Tunnel Prototype

Aerospace Engineering Project

Project Overview

I designed and built a small scale wind tunnel prototype to visualize airflow around miniature vehicle models. The goal was to create a simple system that would allow me to observe how visible mist moved around an F1 car and a Hot Wheels car.

I researched examples of small wind tunnels to understand their general layout, then developed and built my own prototype using mostly low cost materials. The design used a mist source, flow conditioning sections, a clear test section, and an adjustable speed exhaust fan.

The prototype successfully produced airflow through the test section and allowed me to observe mist moving around the vehicle. However, inconsistent mist production and excess water made the visualization difficult to control and prevented reliable repeatable testing.

Objective

Design and build a low cost small scale wind tunnel capable of making airflow visible around miniature vehicle models using mist.

Design & Approach

I started by researching examples of small scale wind tunnels to understand their general layout and airflow path. I then created my own design based on the space available and the components I planned to use.

I chose a clear plastic display box for the test section because it allowed me to see the airflow and made it easy to place and remove the vehicle models. I used a fan at the end of the tunnel to pull air through the system. I also added two flow conditioning sections using coffee stir sticks and boba straws to help guide and stabilize the airflow before and after the test section.

I sketched the design onto cardboard, measured and cut the components, and assembled the prototype myself. The interior was painted black to improve contrast when viewing the mist.

Materials & Construction

I built the prototype primarily from cardboard and a clear plastic display box. I also used a computer fan, mist maker, coffee stir sticks, boba straws, hot glue, spray paint, and an LED light. The vehicle models used for testing were an F1 car and a Hot Wheels car.

I sourced cardboard from recycled packaging and used a box cutter to cut the pieces based on my sketches. I measured the fan so I could design an opening that would fit it, then assembled the cardboard sections around the clear test section.

The final prototype cost approximately $100.

Testing

I tested the prototype using a small F1 model and a Hot Wheels car. The exhaust fan was able to pull air through the tunnel, and the mist generally moved through the test section. I could observe the mist moving around the vehicle models, giving me a basic visual representation of how the airflow interacted with their shapes.

However, the mist generation was inconsistent, which made it difficult to maintain the same conditions during testing. The mist maker also produced excess water that caused the test section and vehicle models to become wet.

Problems & Iterations

The main challenge was producing a consistent mist flow while keeping the test section dry. The mist generator had a small top section designed to sit approximately 4 cm above the water to reduce splashing. At that height, however, the mist output was too weak for the airflow visualization.

I tried removing the top section to increase the mist output, but this caused excessive splashing and made a large mess. I then tried covering the container with plastic to create a more controlled exit path. The plastic collapsed inward, and the mist remained inconsistent. When the mist generator was positioned too close to the plastic, the heat also caused the plastic to melt.

After testing these different approaches, I determined that the mist generation system was the main limitation of the prototype. The excess water and inconsistent mist prevented reliable, repeatable testing, so I decided to discontinue the prototype.

Results

The prototype was able to move air through the test section and produce visible mist around the vehicle models. I observed the mist moving around the contours of the F1 and Hot Wheels cars, demonstrating that the basic airflow visualization concept could work.

However, the prototype was not able to produce the consistent mist conditions needed for reliable testing. Because of the inconsistent mist output and excess water, I was unable to make quantitative measurements or conduct repeatable experiments.

Overall, the prototype demonstrated the basic concept while also showing that the mist generation system needed significant improvement for a future version.

Lessons Learned

This project helped me understand how the placement of the fan and the overall airflow path can affect the flow inside a wind tunnel. I also observed that the airflow tended to follow the contours of the vehicle models, showing me how the shape of an object can influence the path of moving air.

I learned that creating a useful airflow visualization system requires more than simply moving air through a test section. The visualization method also needs to be controlled and consistent so that observations can be repeated and compared.

Future Improvements

If I built a second version, I would first redesign the mist-generation system to produce a more consistent and controlled flow of visible mist while preventing water from entering the test section. I would also improve the overall construction and finishing of the tunnel, including making the interior and exterior cleaner and more polished.

A future version could also focus on creating more controlled testing conditions so that airflow observations could be repeated and compared between different vehicle shapes.
